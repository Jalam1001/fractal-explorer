    What is a fractal?

    A fractal is a shape that keeps showing new detail no matter how closely you look — and very often that detail looks like the shape itself, repeated at a smaller size.

    Draw a circle and zoom in on its edge: soon it looks like a straight line, and zooming further shows nothing new. Do the same with the edge of the Mandelbrot set, a fern leaf in the explorer above, or a rocky coastline on a map, and the edge stays wrinkly. New bumps, spirals and branches keep appearing. Classical geometry (lines, circles, spheres) is about shapes that become simple when magnified; fractal geometry is about shapes that don’t.

    The word fractal was coined in 1975 by the mathematician Benoît Mandelbrot, from the Latin fractus, “broken” or “fragmented”.

    Simple rules, intricate patterns

    The surprise of fractals is how little information they need. The Sierpiński triangle is one instruction — “cut out the middle, then do the same to what’s left” — applied over and over. The Barnsley fern is four short formulas. The Mandelbrot set is a single line: z → z² + c. The complexity is not stored anywhere; it is produced by repetition. Each time the rule is applied, it acts on the result of the previous step, so small effects pile up into structure at every scale. Computers are perfect for this kind of patient repetition, which is why fractals became famous only once computers could draw them.

    Self-similarity: the same, at different scales

    Many fractals are self-similar: a small part, enlarged, resembles the whole. How closely it resembles the whole varies, and that difference matters.

    Exact self-similarity

    Zoom in on the right spot and you get a perfect copy of the whole. The Sierpiński triangle, Koch snowflake, Cantor set and Pythagoras tree are built this way: they are literally made of shrunken copies of themselves.

    Approximate (quasi-) self-similarity

    Parts look like the whole but are distorted or decorated differently. The Mandelbrot set contains infinitely many tiny “minibrots”, yet no two are identical and none is an exact copy of the original. Julia sets and the Burning Ship behave similarly.

    Statistical self-similarity

    No single piece matches the whole, but the roughness does: measured statistically, a stretch of coastline looks as jagged on a 10 km map as on a 100 km map. Mandelbrot’s 1967 paper on coastlines used exactly this idea. Mountains, clouds and fractal terrain in films and games are statistically self-similar.

    A worked example: the Koch curve, recursion and dimension

    Start with a straight segment (generation 0). Apply one rule: replace every straight segment with four segments, each one-third as long, with a triangular bump in the middle. Apply the same rule to every new segment. Doing something to the result of doing the same thing is called recursion — the rule refers to itself.

    The length grows by 4/3 every generation, so the finished curve is infinitely long, yet it fits inside a small box and encloses a finite area when three copies form the snowflake. It is “too big” to be one-dimensional and “too thin” to be two-dimensional. Fractal dimension tells us where it sits in between.

    Measuring dimension by scaling. Cut a line segment into pieces 1/3 as long and you get 3 copies: 3 = 3¹, so a line has dimension 1. Cut a square into pieces 1/3 as wide and you get 9 copies: 9 = 3², dimension 2. A cube gives 27 = 3³, dimension 3. In general, if a shape is made of N copies of itself, each scaled by 1/s, then N = sD, so

    D = log N / log s

    The Koch curve is made of N = 4 copies scaled by 1/3, so D = log 4 / log 3 ≈ 1.2619. By the same reasoning the Sierpiński triangle (3 copies at ½) has D = log 3 / log 2 ≈ 1.585, and the Cantor set (2 copies at ⅓) has D = log 2 / log 3 ≈ 0.631 — more than a scattering of points, less than a line. This “similarity dimension” agrees with the more general Hausdorff dimension for these exactly self-similar shapes; for the Mandelbrot set or a real coastline, dimension has to be measured or proved in other ways.

    Mathematical fractals vs. natural patterns

    In mathematics

    A mathematical fractal is defined by a rule that never stops, so it has detail at arbitrarily small scales. You can zoom into the Koch curve forever and new bumps keep appearing. (On a computer we stop when the pieces are smaller than a pixel — the explorer adds detail automatically as you zoom so you never reach the bottom.)

    In nature

    Real objects are fractal-like only over a limited range of scales. A fern’s leaflets repeat for perhaps three or four levels before you reach individual cells. A coastline is wrinkly from hundreds of kilometres down to rocks and pebbles, but not below the size of sand grains and atoms. Fractals are a model that works very well between those limits.

    Why fractal mathematics matters

    Coastlines and borders

    How long is a coastline? It depends on your ruler. Lewis Fry Richardson found that measured lengths of coasts and borders keep growing as the measuring step shrinks, following a power law. Mandelbrot showed the exponent behaves like a fractional dimension — about 1.25 for the west coast of Britain, against 1.02 for the much smoother coast of South Africa. Fractal dimension gives a single, scale-free number for “how rough” a shape is.

    Branching structures

    Trees, rivers, lightning, blood vessels and lungs all branch, and branch again, over many scales. Human airways split roughly 23 times between the windpipe and the air sacs, packing an enormous surface for gas exchange into the chest. Fractal models of branching help biologists, doctors and engineers describe how such networks fill space and move fluids efficiently — the Pythagoras tree and Barnsley fern above are toy versions of the same idea.

    Computer-generated terrain

    Natural landscapes are statistically self-similar, so they can be faked by adding random bumps at many scales. In 1980 Loren Carpenter showed Vol Libre, a fly-through of fractal mountains, and fractal terrain soon appeared in the “Genesis” sequence of Star Trek II (1982). Fournier, Fussell and Carpenter’s 1982 paper on rendering Mandelbrot’s fractional Brownian motion became a foundation for the procedural landscapes used in films and video games today.

    Fractal ideas also appear in physics (turbulence, the clustering of galaxies), finance (Mandelbrot’s studies of price swings), image compression, antenna design and the analysis of medical images. The common thread: when something is rough or irregular in the same way across many scales, a fractal description captures it with a handful of numbers.

    A short history: from “monsters” to Mandelbrot

    For a long time the shapes we now call fractals were seen as curiosities — “pathological” examples that broke the intuitions of 19th-century calculus. Benoît Mandelbrot’s achievement was to collect them, see them as one family, connect them to the real world, and — with the help of IBM’s computers — show everyone what they look like.

    1. 1872
      Karl Weierstrass presents a function that is continuous everywhere but has no slope (derivative) anywhere: a graph that is jagged at every scale.
    2. 1883
      Georg Cantor publishes the “middle-thirds” set that now carries his name (Henry J. S. Smith had described a similar construction in the 1870s).
    3. 1904
      Helge von Koch describes his snowflake curve: continuous, but without a tangent at any point.
    4. 1915
      Wacław Sierpiński describes the triangle (“gasket”) built by repeatedly removing middle triangles.
    5. 1918
      Felix Hausdorff introduces a notion of dimension that can take fractional values — the mathematical basis of “fractal dimension”. In the same years Gaston Julia (1918) and Pierre Fatou study what happens when a rule such as z → z² + c is repeated — the theory behind Julia sets, worked out entirely without pictures.
    6. 1942
      Dutch engineer and teacher Albert E. Bosman draws the first Pythagoras tree.
    7. 1958
      Benoît Mandelbrot (1924–2010) joins IBM’s research division, at what became the Thomas J. Watson Research Center in Yorktown Heights, New York, where he worked for 35 years. Among his early problems: bursts of noise in data sent over telephone lines, which looked statistically similar whether plotted by the hour or by the second. He also found similar patterns in the price swings of cotton and other markets.
    8. 1961
      Lewis Fry Richardson’s measurements of coastlines and land borders are published, after his death, showing that measured length grows as the ruler shrinks.
    9. 1967
      Mandelbrot’s paper “How Long Is the Coast of Britain? Statistical Self-Similarity and Fractional Dimension” appears in Science. Building on Richardson’s data, it argues that a coastline’s length is not well defined, but its fractional dimension is.
    10. 1975
      Mandelbrot coins the word fractal in his book Les objets fractals: forme, hasard et dimension (English edition 1977).
    11. 1980
      Using IBM computers, Mandelbrot makes and studies detailed pictures of the set now named after him and publishes his findings. (Robert Brooks and Peter Matelski had printed a rough character-based picture of it in 1978.) The same year Loren Carpenter’s fractal film Vol Libre premieres at SIGGRAPH.
    12. 1982
      Mandelbrot’s The Fractal Geometry of Nature is published and brings fractals to a wide audience of scientists, artists and the public. He argued that “clouds are not spheres, mountains are not cones, coastlines are not circles”.
    13. 1988
      Michael Barnsley’s book Fractals Everywhere popularises iterated function systems, including the Barnsley fern.
    14. 1992
      Michael Michelitsch and Otto E. Rössler describe the Burning Ship fractal.

    Mandelbrot later became Sterling Professor of Mathematical Sciences at Yale University. He did not invent most of the individual shapes — that credit belongs to the mathematicians above — but he gave the subject its name, its unifying idea (roughness can be measured), its applications, and its famous images.
